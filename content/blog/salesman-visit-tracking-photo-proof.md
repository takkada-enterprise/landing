A salesman visit tracking app is worth having only if the record it produces was made at the counter, at the moment the visit happened. The daily route report typed at nine in the evening is a memory, and a memory written six hours later always matches the beat plan, because that is the document the salesman copies from. All-day location tracking answers a different question. It records where a phone travelled, which is a fact about a person's whole day including his lunch and his ride home, and field teams resent it enough to leave the phone in a drawer or install something that lies about coordinates. The record that ends an argument is far smaller: one photo taken at the party's counter, carrying the staff member's name, the coordinates the device reported at that second, and the timestamp. This article covers why the evening report proves nothing, what makes a visit record hold up, and what an owner does with a month of them.

## What a Salesman Visit Tracking App Has to Prove

Ask a distributor why he wants a salesman visit tracking app and he will not describe a dashboard. He will describe a specific Tuesday. A retailer four hours away told him nobody from the company had come in two months. His salesman's report for that period lists that shop four times. One of them is wrong, and he has no way to find out which.

That is the entire brief. The question is narrow and factual: was this person at this shop on this day. Coverage, beat discipline, which parties are being skipped: all of it rests on that one answer being trustworthy. If the underlying record can be produced from a chair, nothing built on it means anything.

This is a different job from the one an order-taking app does. A [field order collection app for Tally](/blog/field-order-collection-app-tally/) proves a visit whenever an order comes out of it, because the order is a fact created at the counter. In a slow month most visits produce no order, and those are the visits an owner doubts.

## Why the Evening Route Report Proves Nothing

The standard field report is a list of shops sent on WhatsApp at the end of the day, sometimes from home. It has three structural problems, and none of them are about honesty.

The first is that it is written from the plan rather than from the day. The salesman opens his beat list, copies the names, adds a remark against each. Even an honest man reconstructs: he believes he stopped at all eleven shops, and he can no longer separate the one he only drove past.

The second is that every report looks identical. Eleven shops, "stock check", "will order next week", "party out of station". Nothing in it could only have been produced by being present, and a report a man could write from his sofa says nothing about where he wrote it.

The third is that it cannot be checked without calling the retailer, and checking on your own staff that way is something owners do once before deciding it costs more than it is worth.

## Following Him All Day, or Stamping the Moment of the Visit

The market's answer has been continuous location tracking: a background service reporting the phone's position every few minutes and drawing a line on a map. It produces data. The cost is worth stating plainly.

| | Continuous location tracking | A stamped visit record |
|---|---|---|
| What it captures | Where the phone went all day | The moment the salesman stood at the counter |
| What the salesman feels | Followed, including on his lunch and his route home | Asked to log the visit he just made |
| Battery cost | All day, on his own phone | A few seconds per shop |
| What it shows in a dispute | The phone passed near the shop | A photo of the shop carrying a name, coordinates and a time |
| How it fails | The service is killed, the phone dies, the location is spoofed | The visit does not get logged, and the gap is visible |

The morale cost gets underweighted, and it is not a sentimental point. A field salesman holds the relationship with your retailer. Telling him you will watch his position from morning to night changes what he thinks the company thinks of him, and the good ones have options. A stamped record asks for something much narrower. Prove the eight minutes you are claiming credit for, and the rest of your day belongs to you.

Background location is also the first thing a phone's power manager kills, so a breadcrumb trail carries holes that look exactly like a salesman who went home early. A record created by a deliberate action at the counter either exists or does not.

## The Four Facts That Make a Visit Record Hold Up

A visit record that survives being questioned carries four things, together, on one artefact.

**The photograph.** Taken at the counter, showing the shop and often the retailer standing in it. It is the only one of the four a retailer will recognise as his own premises when it is shown back to him.

**The staff member's name.** The record has to say who was holding the phone, because a shared device with no attribution replaces the old argument with a new one.

**The coordinates the device reported.** Captured at the instant of the photo, not typed, not chosen from a list.

**The timestamp.** The hour, so a beat reads as a sequence and not just a set.

These four have to travel together, printed on the image rather than sitting in a database column beside it, because field evidence gets forwarded. It goes into a family WhatsApp group, into a sales review, into a conversation with the retailer himself. A photo carrying its own caption plate stays true after somebody screenshots it. A photo whose details lived somewhere else becomes just a photo of a shop.

## Being Honest About What the Phone Actually Knew

Two details separate a visit record built by someone who thought about being wrong from one built to look reassuring.

The first is the address. A device reports latitude and longitude, and that is the fact worth storing. The street address a human wants to read is produced later by looking those coordinates up, and in a Tier 3 town that lookup will confidently name the wrong lane often enough to matter. So the coordinates are kept, the address is derived at the moment somebody views the record, and it is labelled approximate on the plate. The owner still reads a place name, and he is told plainly how much weight it can take.

The second is the case where the location cannot be trusted at all. The salesman denied the permission. The phone got no fix inside a concrete godown. The device reported a position a mock-location app supplied. A system built to look good treats all three as a blank field and shows the visit like any other. A system built to be believed marks that visit unverified and leaves the photo, the name and the time standing.

That choice is uncomfortable, because the owner will see unverified rows and ask why. It is also the only reason the verified rows mean anything. Every record that claims certainty it does not have devalues the records that earned it.

## What an Owner Does With a Month of Visit Records

The word tracking pulls the mind towards catching someone, and that is the smallest possible use of this data. Owners who get value out of visit records use them for coverage.

A month of stamped visits answers questions the sales register cannot. Which of my 180 parties has not been physically visited in six weeks. Which parties get a weekly visit and still do not order, so a good salesman's time is going into a dead relationship. Which of my two towns is actually being worked, when the [salesman-wise sales report](/blog/salesman-wise-sales-report-tally/) shows both at similar numbers.

The strongest pairing is visits against money. Read a party's visit history next to its [outstanding statement](/blog/partywise-outstanding-statement-tally/) and a familiar pattern appears: the parties with the oldest bills are frequently the ones nobody has stood in front of in two months. Overdue money and absent salesmen are the same problem seen from two ends, and neither report shows it alone.

Used this way the record stops being a stick and becomes the input to next month's beat plan, and the salesman who genuinely runs his territory is the one it helps most.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Does a salesman visit tracking app need to track location all day?**

A: No, and most field teams are better served if it does not. All-day tracking watches the whole day to prove eight minutes at a counter, drains the salesman's own battery, and gets killed by the phone before the day ends anyway. A record created at the moment of the visit, carrying a photo, a name, coordinates and a time, proves what the owner asked about and leaves the rest of the day alone.

**Q: What stops a salesman from taking the visit photo somewhere else?**

A: The coordinates are captured by the device as the photo is taken and printed on the record, so a photo taken from home carries coordinates that do not match the shop. The photo is the second check, because a retailer recognises his own counter. Where the device reports a position that cannot be trusted, including a spoofed one, the visit should be marked unverified rather than shown as confirmed.

**Q: Why is the address on the visit photo marked approximate?**

A: Because the device measures latitude and longitude, not an address. The address is produced afterwards by looking those coordinates up, and in smaller towns that lookup often names a nearby lane rather than the exact one. Storing the coordinates as the fact and labelling the derived address approximate tells the owner how much weight the place name carries, which beats a confident address that is quietly wrong.

**Q: What happens when the phone cannot get a location at the shop?**

A: The visit is still recorded, with the photo, the staff member's name and the timestamp intact, and the location is flagged as unverified. A system that hides failed location checks makes all of its verified visits unprovable, because there is no longer any difference between a location that was confirmed and one that was never obtained.

**Q: Will my salesmen object to visit tracking?**

A: They object to being followed all day, and that objection is usually reasonable. One stamped record per shop is a smaller ask, and the salesmen who genuinely run their beat tend to like it, because their coverage finally appears in a document the owner reads. Tell the team what is captured before switching it on, since a tracking system introduced quietly is the one that gets worked around.

**Q: Is a sales order enough proof that the visit happened?**

A: It is good proof when an order exists, since an order booked at the counter could not have been created from home. The gap is that in a weak month most visits produce no order, and those are the visits an owner doubts. A visit record covers the shops where nothing was bought, and that is where coverage is won or lost.
